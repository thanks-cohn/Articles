God’s Greatest Fear

Well, do you think God’s greatest fear is a world full of death, disease, and hunger?

Please.

He seems remarkably composed about those.

No, perhaps the Almighty is afraid of something far more dangerous.

A good life.

It does not take a lengthy inspection of human history to notice that, if an all-knowing and all-powerful God has exercised any influence here, He has been strangely partial to a frightened, bleeding, terminally ill world.

And why not?

Misery is wonderful advertising.

A starving man will listen to anyone holding bread. A grieving mother will entertain promises she once would have laughed out of the room. A terrified child can be taught to call almost any shelter holy, provided the storm outside is bad enough.

But a happy man?

There is the real theological emergency.

A man who wakes beside someone he loves, whose children are healthy, whose work has meaning, whose table has enough, and whose conscience does not tremble before invisible judges might begin asking a very dangerous question:

What exactly am I being saved from?

It is much harder to sell spiritual dependency to people who do not despise being alive.

Hell loses some of its bargaining power when Earth is not already serving as the demonstration model. Heaven becomes a less dazzling prize when a mother’s smile, a father’s kiss, and an ordinary Sunday afternoon already feel sufficient.

What could defeat God more completely in the human heart than contentment?

Not rebellion.

Not blasphemy.

Not some horned devil waving a pitchfork.

Just breakfast with the family.

Just a body that does not ache.

Just children who are not buried.

Just enough money, enough time, enough love, and no desperate need to believe that suffering must secretly mean something.

Perhaps that is why religion so often arrives beside the hospital bed, the coffin, the prison cell, and the battlefield. It calls this compassion, naturally. But one cannot help noticing that the salesman always seems to appear precisely when the customer is least able to refuse.

And then comes the offer:

Your life is broken.

The world is fallen.

You are empty.

You need Him.

Convenient, isn’t it?

Because a good life might suggest something absolutely catastrophic.

That man can be kind without surveillance.

That love can exist without command.

That gratitude can arise without debt.

That meaning can grow without permission.

That people might hold one another through the darkness and discover, to everyone’s embarrassment, that no throne was required.

Perhaps God does not fear a world that rejects Him.

Perhaps He fears a world that no longer needs Him.

A world so gentle, so healthy, so abundantly human that the threat of hell sounds vulgar, the promise of heaven sounds redundant, and the divine salesman finds himself standing outside a happy home, knocking and knocking, while no one inside can hear Him over the laughter.
