HERE FALLS MY SOUL

Every Thought a Crime

There is something morbid about teaching a child that every feeling is a prophecy.

He is angry, therefore somewhere beneath his ribs sleeps a murderer.

He is jealous, therefore he is already a thief.

He looks at Becky for half a second too long and suddenly heaven has convened an emergency tribunal.

Good Lord.

He is sixteen.

Of course he gets angry. Martha burned the Hot Pockets again. This is hardly the prelude to genocide.

Everyone feels rage. Everyone envies. Everyone has some hideous little thought dart through the mind, uninvited and absurd, then disappear before it has even found somewhere to sit. The human brain produces strange material. Dreams, impulses, grudges, fantasies, fears. Most of it means nothing. Some of it means you are tired. Some of it means John has been unbearable all afternoon.

But give religion access to a frightened young mind and suddenly every passing shadow becomes evidence for the prosecution.

"I really wanted to hurt John."

Then you must be a murderer.

"I looked at Becky today."

An adulterer.

"I enjoyed it."

God help us. A monster.

And if the poor boy should discover the sock and the screen, well, clear the sanctuary. Satan himself has entered the Wi-Fi password.

The joke would be harmless if children were not actually taught to fear themselves this way.

They begin examining every thought as though it arrived carrying a divine warrant. They inspect every feeling, then inspect themselves for having inspected it incorrectly. Was that anger? Was it hatred? Did I enjoy the hatred? Did I enjoy remembering that I enjoyed it? Have I repented sincerely enough? Was I secretly proud of repenting?

Round and round they go, polishing the bars of a prison no one else can see.

This is scrupulosity, and it is a wicked thing. Not because the person suffering from it is wicked, but because it can turn conscience into a torture device. A decent young person becomes terrified that decency is merely the costume worn by the beast inside him. The gentler he is, the more carefully he searches for hidden cruelty. The more honest he tries to become, the more suspicious he grows of every honest answer.

He cannot prove he is innocent because the court has moved inside his skull, where evidence may be invented forever.

And what a useful court it is.

A deed can be examined. A victim can be found. Harm can be measured. But once thoughts themselves become crimes, everyone is guilty and no acquittal is possible. The church need not wait for you to do something evil. It may prosecute the imagination, and the imagination never keeps a clean record.

That is the trick.

First, erase the difference between thinking and doing.

Then point to the resulting terror as proof that man is hopelessly corrupt.

Finally, offer the institution as the only refuge from the monster it taught the child to see in the mirror.

Invent the disease. Sell the cure. Call the customer a sheep so often that nobody notices how eagerly you need the wool.

The tragedy is that the distinction between thought and deed is not some moral technicality. It is the very ground of moral life.

A good man is not a man who has never imagined striking someone. Such a creature would not be virtuous. He would be furniture.

A good man feels anger and does not strike. He experiences desire and still respects another person's boundaries. He knows envy without becoming a thief. He discovers that his mind is capable of ugliness, absurdity, and appetite, then chooses what kind of person his hands will make him.

The choice is the virtue.

Erase the choice and you erase the man. You replace character with contamination. You tell him that resisting cruelty means nothing because imagining cruelty already made him guilty. Then, when he behaves decently anyway, you credit God for restraining the homicidal animal you insist he is.

How convenient.

His goodness becomes God's achievement. His thoughts become his crime.

The house always wins.

So the boy comes forward.

He is fourteen, perhaps. Or seventeen. Or thirty-five and still apologizing for having once been fourteen. His hands shake. His face burns. He confesses anger that harmed no one, desire that violated no one, and thoughts that frightened only him.

"Here falls my soul," he says.

And the church receives it with solemn delight.

Another sinner saved.

Saved from what?

From becoming a murderer because John annoyed him?

From ravaging the countryside because Becky looked lovely?

From the catastrophic discovery that pleasure sometimes feels pleasant?

No. He has been saved from trusting his own mind. Saved from believing that an impulse may pass without becoming destiny. Saved from the dangerous knowledge that he can feel something fierce, strange, sexual, selfish, or furious and still choose not to hurt anyone.

In other words, he has been saved from moral adulthood.

Now he has shepherds.

They need young boys. Ahem, young men. No, forgive me. Sheep.

And sheep must distrust the wilderness within. They must believe that beyond the shepherd's crook lies instant savagery. Otherwise they may look around and notice that most people, most days, manage not to stab everything in front of them without once consulting a pastor.

Perhaps man is not good because every thought within him is pure.

Perhaps he is good because they are not.

Perhaps goodness is what happens when a complicated animal, full of appetite and anger and imagination, refuses to make another person pay for every storm passing through his head.

That is not depravity.

That is civilization.

So stop demanding purity from the involuntary theater of the mind. Judge yourself by what you nurture, what you choose, what you do, and what you repair when you fail. If a thought troubles you, let it trouble you and pass. It is not a confession merely because you heard it.

You were not secretly a murderer.

John was irritating.

You were not spiritually diseased.

Becky was beautiful.

You were not proof of original sin.

You were young, frightened, alive, and far more decent than the people who taught you to mistake fear for holiness.

Your soul did not need to fall into their hands.

It was never evidence.
