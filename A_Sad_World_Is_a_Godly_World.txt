A SAD WORLD IS A GODLY WORLD

Depression, Seasoned to Taste


Arthur Brooks would like to explain why everyone is miserable.

Social media. Loneliness. Politics. Bad habits. The collapse of community. Too much comparison, not enough gratitude, and almost certainly something involving your phone before breakfast.

All very respectable.

Charts may be produced. Columns may be written. A podcast may even be endured.

But has anyone considered a less respectable possibility?

Perhaps the Lord is making everyone miserable.

Now, I am not claiming to know what God wants.

Christians have already monopolized that profession.

I am only borrowing their premises for a moment.

They tell us that God knows every thought, enters every heart, governs every age, permits every trial, appoints every season, and may use suffering to draw His beloved creatures toward Him.

Very well.

Then perhaps He has drawn unusually close.

Perhaps, like a child who cannot bear to see the other children enjoying themselves without him, the Almighty has overturned the table, dimmed the lights, and left a little depression seasoned throughout the meal.

Not too much.

Just enough to make everyone ask who turned off the sun.

Christianity has spent centuries announcing that a godless world is a sad world. Remove God, we are told, and meaning withers. Joy becomes hollow. Families collapse. Men lose their purpose. Women lose something else. The children become confused. Eventually everyone is depressed, tattooed, and listening to a podcast about the death of civilization.

But perhaps the order has been reversed.

Perhaps a godless world did not become sad.

Perhaps the world was made sad so that godlessness could be blamed.

Glory, hallelujah.

The Lord is working.

Affects their minds?

Clouds their thoughts?

Leaves them unable to enjoy the lives He so generously gave them?

Praise His mysterious name.

A little dread in the morning. A little emptiness after lunch. A little voice at night insisting that nothing will ever feel right again.

Marvelous.

The Spirit moves in mysterious neurological ways.

Then, when the exhausted creature finally collapses and cries out to God, the church may point to the wreckage and shout:

“See? He needed the Lord!”

Needed Him?

My dear brother, he needed sleep.

Still, why allow medicine, circumstance, loneliness, grief, poverty, or a collapsing society to steal God’s glory? If the sadness eventually produces a prayer, surely heaven deserves some credit for the sadness.

Otherwise God merely arrives after the assault and takes a photograph with the survivor.

No, give Him the whole testimony.

“I was happy once. Secure. Content. Then the Lord placed His hand upon my life. I stopped sleeping. Food lost its taste. I withdrew from everyone I loved. One night, on the bathroom floor, I finally called His name.”

What a beautiful story.

If a man did that to you, we would call it psychological abuse.

Put a halo around it and suddenly it is providence.

Again, I do not know whether God wants this.

Neither do they.

That is what makes the Christian confidence so entertaining. They will identify God’s hand in a parking space, a promotion, a recovered wallet, and a perfectly timed rainbow. But suggest that the same sovereign hand might be somewhere near the despair, and suddenly theology becomes very cautious.

Who can know the mind of God?

Apparently everyone, until the crime scene.

Still, does the Bible itself present God as unwilling to interfere with the human mind?

Hmm.

He hardens hearts. He sends a lying spirit. He permits a tormenting spirit to descend upon Saul. He blinds, confuses, deludes, and hands people over to minds that no longer function as they should. The biblical interior life is not private property. God may enter, rearrange the furniture, turn off the lights, and leave the tenant responsible for whatever happens next.

But surely He would never tamper with your mind.

That would be invasive.

No, no. He merely knows every thought before you think it, judges each desire as it forms, and may alter the conditions under which you choose.

Privacy itself must be one of those godless modern inventions.

And what if some people die?

Depression does that.

People vanish into bedrooms, hospitals, bottles, and graves. Families spend the rest of their lives wondering what they failed to notice. Children inherit silences they cannot name.

A terrible price.

But if the misery frightens millions back toward the altar?

Well.

One soul for many?

Hardly a price.

So says the Lord, at least when ventriloquized by someone who is not volunteering to pay it.

Christian arithmetic becomes remarkably economical once eternity is introduced. A body is temporary. A soul is forever. Therefore any finite agony can be placed beneath an infinite good and made to look almost free.

A few minds must break so that many knees may bend.

A few people must disappear so that the survivors will search for meaning.

A few mothers must learn to cry quietly so that someone may later call their endurance a testimony.

Praise God.

No waste in His kingdom.

Eventually people begin saying the sentence the whole arrangement seems designed to produce:

“You know, things used to be good way back then.”

There it is.

Not quite a memory. More like an advertisement wearing your grandmother’s perfume.

The past becomes warmer with every miserable year. The church bells sound sweeter. The fathers were stronger. The mothers were happier. Everyone believed, everyone belonged, and nobody was depressed, except for all the people who were and had the decency not to ruin the photograph.

Soon nostalgia performs its little resurrection.

Faith was present during happier times.

Therefore faith must have caused them.

Godless life is miserable.

Life is miserable.

Therefore, you must need God.

One hardly needs Arthur Brooks after that.

To be clear, perhaps there is no divine conspiracy. That remains the considerably saner possibility. Human beings are perfectly capable of building a cruel, lonely world without supernatural assistance.

This is not an accusation from revelation.

It is a mock sermon assembled from Christian claims.

If God governs all things, reaches into minds, uses suffering, values souls above bodies, and longs to draw humanity toward Himself, then misery fits rather neatly inside the machinery. The believer may reject that conclusion, naturally. He may call it blasphemous, unfair, simplistic, and cruel.

But he cannot call it foreign.

The parts came from his own box.

The point is not that we know God caused depression.

The point is that Christianity has constructed a God who could, supplied Him with precedent, declared His motives loving by definition, and then forbidden the patient from questioning the treatment.

If the mind clears, God healed it.

If the mind clouds, God is testing it.

If the sufferer returns to church, God called him home.

If the sufferer dies, God’s ways are higher than ours.

Heads He wins.

Tails you receive a casserole.

Perhaps God has nothing to do with any of it.

But then perhaps the church should stop using human misery as evidence that we have wandered from Him. It cannot point to the wound as proof that we need the very being it insists controls the knife.

Unless, of course, that is the sermon.

Affects their minds.

Clouds their thoughts.

Leaves the whole age vaguely exhausted, frightened, and nostalgic for an imaginary past.

Glory, hallelujah.

A happy species might forget to kneel. A peaceful mind might resent being searched. A person who enjoys life without divine permission may conclude that joy requires neither blood nor debt nor an eternal audience.

That is the truly dangerous world.

Not a godless world.

A godless world that is doing just fine.

And if it is doing just fine?

Well.

Add a little depression.

Season to taste.
