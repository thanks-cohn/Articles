A SAD WORLD IS A GODLY WORLD

Depression, Seasoned to Taste


Arthur Brooks would like to explain why everyone is miserable.

Social media. Loneliness. Politics. Bad habits. The collapse of community. Not enough gratitude, too much comparison, and probably something involving your phone before breakfast.

All very respectable.

Charts may be produced. Columns may be written. A podcast may even be endured.

But has anyone considered the more obvious possibility?

Perhaps the Lord is making everyone miserable.

I know. Ridiculous.

An all-loving God would never poison the emotional atmosphere merely because people stopped paying attention to Him. He is not some spoiled child who, upon discovering that the other children are having fun without him, kicks over the table and announces that nobody is having a birthday anymore.

Surely not.

Still, one cannot help wondering.

Christianity has spent centuries insisting that a godless world is a sad world. Remove God, we are told, and meaning withers. Joy becomes hollow. Families collapse. Men lose their purpose. Women lose something else. The children become confused. Eventually everyone is depressed, tattooed, and listening to a podcast in which two strangers discuss the death of civilization.

But perhaps the order has been reversed.

Perhaps a godless world did not become sad.

Perhaps the world was made sad so that godlessness could be blamed.

Now there is a miracle worthy of the name.

Break a man’s legs, then explain that he cannot walk without you. Empty his house, then wait outside with a loaf of bread. Creep into the private rooms of his mind, disturb the furniture, leave a little despair beneath the pillow, and when he wakes exhausted, whisper:

“See what life is like without Me?”

Divine marketing has always possessed a certain muscular elegance.

After all, happiness is dangerous.

A genuinely contented person may not feel the proper urgency. He may wake beside someone he loves, enjoy his breakfast, go to work, laugh with his friends, come home tired, and forget entirely that an invisible monarch is waiting to be thanked.

This cannot be allowed to become a habit.

So perhaps the Lord does not ruin everything. That would be too obvious. Perhaps He merely seasons the world.

A little dread in the morning.

A little emptiness after lunch.

A little suspicion that nothing will ever feel quite right again.

Not enough to leave fingerprints. Just enough to make the sermon sound plausible.

Then, when an entire generation begins to sag beneath the weight of it, respectable men arrive to explain the crisis. We have lost meaning. We have abandoned tradition. We have wandered too far from God.

How fortunate that the crime scene came with a business card.

I am joking, naturally.

Depression is an illness, not a thunderbolt hurled by an offended deity. Human misery has causes far less theatrical than celestial sulking.

But before the Christian laughs too loudly, let us ask an awkward little question.

Does the Bible claim that God interferes with people’s minds?

Hmm.

He hardens hearts. He sends a lying spirit. He permits a tormenting spirit to descend upon Saul. He blinds, confuses, deludes, and hands people over to minds that no longer function as they should. The sacred text does not treat the interior life as private property. God enters whenever He pleases, rearranges the motives, dims the lights, and leaves the tenant responsible for whatever happens next.

But surely He would never tamper with your mind.

That would be invasive.

And what if some people die?

Depression does that.

People disappear into bedrooms, hospitals, bottles, and graves. Families spend the rest of their lives asking what they failed to notice. Children inherit silences they cannot name.

A dreadful price.

Unless, of course, misery drives millions back toward the altar.

Then the arithmetic becomes theological.

One life for many souls?

Hardly a price.

So says the Lord, usually through someone who has not volunteered to pay it.

Christianity has never been terribly frightened by a corpse when eternity can be placed on the other side of the scale. A body is temporary. A soul is forever. Once infinity enters the calculation, any finite suffering can be declared economical.

A few must break so that many may kneel.

A few must vanish so that the survivors may search for meaning.

A few mothers must learn to cry into pillows so that a congregation can later call their endurance beautiful.

Waste not, want not.

And eventually people begin saying the sentence the whole arrangement appears designed to produce:

“You know, things used to be good way back then.”

There it is.

Not a memory, exactly. More like an advertisement wearing your grandmother’s perfume.

The past becomes warmer with every miserable year. The church bells sound sweeter. The fathers were stronger. The mothers were happier. Everyone believed, everyone belonged, and nobody was depressed, except for all the people who were and had the decency not to ruin the photograph.

Soon nostalgia does what terror could not. It persuades people that faith was not merely present during happier times, but responsible for them.

The trap closes softly.

Godless life is miserable.

Life is miserable.

Therefore, you must need God.

One hardly needs Arthur Brooks after that.

Perhaps there is no divine conspiracy. That remains the considerably saner possibility. Perhaps human beings built a cruel, lonely world all by themselves and then gave heaven the credit, as usual.

But if we are going to entertain the Christian God on Christianity’s own terms, we should at least grant Him the powers His admirers insist He possesses. He knows every mind. He enters every heart. He commands spirits, delusions, peace, terror, consolation, and despair.

The only missing ingredient is motive.

And what motive could an all-loving God possibly have for making His creatures miserable?

Worship.

A happy species might forget to kneel.

A peaceful mind might resent being searched.

A person who enjoys life without permission may conclude that joy requires neither blood nor debt nor an eternal audience.

That is the truly dangerous world.

Not a godless world.

A godless world that is doing just fine.

Perhaps God does not need everyone dead, diseased, or howling in the streets. He only needs life to hurt enough that people mistake relief for revelation. Just enough sorrow to make yesterday holy. Just enough loneliness to make surveillance feel like company. Just enough despair to make surrender resemble hope.

Then, when humanity finally crawls back and asks why everything became so unbearable, the Lord may open His arms with the wounded dignity of a father whose children forced Him to hurt them.

A godless world is a sad world.

Or so He wishes us to believe.

And if it is not sad?

Add a little depression.

Season to taste.
